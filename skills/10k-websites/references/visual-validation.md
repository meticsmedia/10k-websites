# Visual validation before expanding production

Read while preparing keyframes and before animating a recurring subject or scene. Use these checks to decide whether an asset is ready to propagate into other views, formats and films. They are internal review steps within the existing creative and spending authorization, not additional user approval gates.

## Establish what must remain true

For a recurring product, person or place, select authoritative references and record the defining traits in the design package. Keep intentional story changes separate from invariants. Independent scenes do not need identical contents simply because they share an art direction.

Record only consequential details: object count and design, proportions, materials, orientation, relative spacing, connections and landmarks. For a place, a rough plan or annotated reference can clarify what is beside, behind or separate from what. Describe these relationships in the scene's coordinates; camera-left can change when the camera moves. A matching object count alone does not establish consistent geometry.

Review each view against that record. Mark a critical trait **matched**, **intentionally changed**, **occluded**, or **uncertain**, and record any mismatch to repair. Perspective and occlusion can explain appearance differences; do not demand identical screen positions or pretend hidden details were verified. Resolve unexplained critical differences before using the images as animation endpoints. Use another authoritative view to check occluded objects when their later appearance matters.

## Assign reference roles

For a local correction, use one image as the camera/composition authority. Give additional references specific roles such as object identity, material or botanical detail. Preserve the established viewpoint and unaffected geometry. Mixing aerial and eye-level references without those roles can produce a collage instead of one coherent camera view.

For a new viewpoint, state which composition changes and which scene relationships remain fixed. An edit that improves texture still needs the full continuity check: it may have moved furniture, changed a product component or invented another object. Preserve the original and record the selected version.

## Inspect the hardest detail first

Identify the subject's likely failure points: foliage and branch connections, fabric weave, lettering, anatomy, repeated components or another defining detail. Inspect representative crops at native resolution and in the intended desktop/phone presentation before generating dependent variants. Deliberate depth of field and motion blur are valid; unintended blur or invented structure that obscures the subject is a defect.

Compare the source, web encode and rendered page to locate softness before choosing a repair. Compression, resizing and CSS effects need different fixes from an already-fuzzy master. Compare models or edits using corresponding, matched-scale crops without added sharpening; disclose reframing that prevents a like-for-like comparison. Judge detail together with identity and composition, not in isolation.

If a conservative edit preserves the same defect, change the relevant method: a clearer detail reference, a bounded region replacement or a better-suited model within the approved plan. Repeating 'sharper' or requesting more pixels is not evidence of restored detail. Do not invent a universal model ranking from one successful subject, or redesign a real product under the guise of retouching it.

## Prove the movement before expanding it

For an ambitious, unproven route, record observable motion: starting distance/height, direction and scale change, foreground parallax, any traversable opening or boundary, arrival bearing, and where acceleration, deceleration or a reading hold belongs. Adapt this to subject transformations when the subject drives the story. A distant opening can deliberately keep the destination small.

Use one representative motion pilot before committing dependent segments and formats when the unresolved route is a material production risk. Choose the format that best exposes that risk, using the approved model/quality and allowance. An already-reviewed representative clip can serve as the pilot; do not require a redundant paid test. Independent stills and site work can continue meanwhile.

Watch the actual motion at normal speed, inspect suspect moments at half speed or frame by frame, and test forward/reverse scrubbing. Check that the camera travels along the intended route, solid objects remain anchored unless the story moves them, the subject stays coherent, and a boundary crossing is legible. An extended opaque smear, early arrival or internal stop should not pass merely because the first and last frames look right.

Choose a continuous take or a chain using the motion and model's actual capabilities; see [shots.md](shots.md) and [prompt-laws.md](prompt-laws.md). A pinned endpoint constrains a picture, not its incoming/outgoing velocity. Compare motion near joins. Reserve a resting endpoint for the intended final arrival; use a guide image rather than a mandatory internal endpoint when the chosen method needs uninterrupted passage. Resolve repeated stopping by reconsidering the take structure, not by silently trimming or retiming the approved story.

## Record acceptance and recheck affected dependencies

Keep brief evidence with the selected source/version: difficult-detail crops, continuity findings, observed route and timing, and any accepted limitation. A contact sheet supports review but does not prove motion. Check the selected full film and relevant transitions, not just promising samples.

Replacing a keyframe requires rechecking affected sibling views, joins, portrait assets, posters and film-linked typography. Mark those dependencies as pending until checked; do not present a correction as complete while the preview still uses the old asset. Update the review page and production manifest to the same selected versions. Preserve unaffected accepted work, and follow [production.md](production.md) before spending on repairs.
